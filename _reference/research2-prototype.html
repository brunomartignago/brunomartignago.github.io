Entry 01

Context

This is the second part of a research project I've been working on since 2022. The first part focused on what a page is made of. This one focuses on how people move through it.

Let's go back to our axiom. We defined a web page as the combination of two pillars: visuals and content, and demographics and culture. From the first two studies, we can already understand the first pillar. The second is still too hard to put into metrics.

We already know that the information on screen plays a big role in guiding us through the "flow" of navigation. But how, exactly?

Part 1“Visual patterns in web page generation” covers studies 1 and 2: the Form, Function, Component method and template summarization.
02
Entry 02 · Hypothesis

A page has weight

My hypothesis: people perceive a web page the way they perceive a physical object. Dense areas weigh more, and weight pulls attention.

To test it, a flat page needed a third dimension. The information density mapping algorithm detects feature points on a screenshot, counts them in a grid of 50 × 50 px cells, and uses that count as the Z axis. The page becomes a terrain.

Cognitive Load IndexThe same algorithm scores a page from 0 to 100, from cluster distance, cluster count and whitespace ratio (15% optimal). Lenovo's cart scored 59.2.

Entry 03

Center of mass 101

This is an excuse for a mathematician to talk about math. In a continuous system, the center of mass is every position weighted by its density, divided by the total mass. Our pages are a grid of cells, so the integrals become sums:

CMx = Σ mi · xiΣ miCMy = Σ mi · yiΣ mi

In plain words: it's the point where the page would balance on a fingertip if it were printed on a sheet of uneven thickness. Try it below.

Local vs globalWe calculated it for the whole page and for each 1920 × 1080 screen, because a visitor only ever sees one screen at a time.
04
Entry 04 · Method

The eye-tracking study

Third round of tests

2024 · iMotions · 14 participants via UserTesting.com
  1. Select 6 random pages from the second round: 2 item detail, 2 item list and 2 cart pages, 3 long and 3 short.
  2. Calculate every local center of mass for each 1920 × 1080 screen.
  3. Run an eye-tracking test with no pre-determined tasks: 60 seconds per page.
  4. Compare the centers of mass with the results.

No task was on purpose. A task tells people where to look. We wanted to see where the page itself takes them.

We ran the test in iMotions with my research partner, behavioral scientist Annamarie Huttunen, who developed the density mapping and analysis, operated the software and collaborated on the findings. I had the theory; she had the tools.

Why these pagesThey come from study 2's sample, so each one already had its Form, Function, Component classification.
05
Entry 05 · Finding

Gravity

The first and most important finding: people are drawn towards the center of mass, especially in longer and more complicated pages. The hottest areas of attention cluster around the circle drawn at each screen's center, and the per-area look counts and timelines show the gaze fixating and looping around it.

06
Entry 06 · Finding

Movement

On long pages, the gaze doesn't scan evenly from top to bottom. It travels. Each screen has its own center of mass, and attention moves from one to the next, like stepping stones down the page.

07
Entry 07 · Finding

Assimilation span

Short pages behave differently. People start paying attention to the content only after realizing there isn't more of the page to explore. The time between their first observation and the read-through is the assimilation span.

On a long page the pattern never completes: people observe the top for 15 seconds, then the gaze only dissipates.

Same page, same peopleThe Lenovo maps for 0–20 s and 40–60 s look like two different studies.
08
Entry 08 · Finding

Attractors

Gravity explained where people look, not how they move between those places. We divided each page into a grid of 36 areas of interest (columns A–F, rows 0–5). Mapping the first 18 by hit time showed three things: clockwise motion around the center of mass, dissipation when the gaze sits between two centers of mass, and focal groups around each center.

Revisits told the same story: the gaze cycles, and revisits and dwell time are directly proportional. One outlier: E1 has around 50% fewer revisits than B1, but the same dwell time.

This looks a lot like an attractor model: a ball rolling over a surface with two wells. It orbits, crosses between them, and eventually settles.

Lorenz attractorA set of chaotic solutions where tiny changes in the starting point evolve into completely different paths. Our gaze paths share that shape.
09
Entry 09

What's next

In all honesty, this is a beginning, not a conclusion. Six pages and 14 people are enough to see a pattern, not to prove a law. But the data is promising, and we are ready for bigger experiments.

If attention behaves like gravity, a layout isn't only arranged. It's balanced.

Our main objective is to capture enough data to create an AI that can generate great page and flow design: one that knows not only what goes on a page, but where people's eyes will go.
