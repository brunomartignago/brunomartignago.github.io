Entry 01

Context

This is a research project I've been working on for the last four years. The focus was on understanding the core pillars that constitute a web page. This understanding would eventually pave the way for creating an algorithm for webpage and flow generation via AI training.

This is the first part of a large set of findings from 5 tests conducted from 2022 to 2025. This case focuses on results that address element placement and the development of a universal design–development taxonomy, enabling web templates that facilitate discovery research and speed it up.

Entry 02

Introduction

For this project, I worked with a base definition, an axiom, that we will use throughout the entire study. It's important to have this definition made very early in the conversation, so all the knots are tied to this truth.

We will define a webpage as a visual structure that is designed from a combination of two pillars:

AxiomThe one definition everything else in this study is tied back to.
A

Graphic components

content · low variation · follows rules
B

Culture

user demographics · high variation

These pillars sustain the entire structure of a webpage and guide designers, developers, and product people in making decisions when building these solutions.

03
Entry 03

Research questions

We want to better understand what makes people navigate the way they navigate, how they decide what to look at on a page, and what helps them find the information they're looking for. This is the part where AI-generated pages fail when exposed to users: they look good, but there's no appeal outside of the visual layer.

With these considerations, I had two questions in mind that I wanted to understand with this research:

Q1

If demographics are fixed, would it be possible to map patterns of visual elements on pages and flows?

Q2

Is it possible to simplify web page structuring so it's easier to automate?

Method noteCulture is treated as a constant: each test came with a pre-selected set of demographic information.

Entry 04 · Study 1

The first study

In the first test, our main objective was to understand how to classify the elements within a page, their visual properties, and their relative positions on the screen. Our sample was formed by the following:

First test guidelines

31 pages · most-used websites in Brazil

5 page types

  • Home
  • Item list
  • Item detail
  • Article
  • User page

6 content types

  • E-commerce
  • News
  • Social media
  • Streaming
  • Weather
  • Services

To compare pages of different sizes, we needed a uniform categorization of page sections. We built it through a series of template section definitions, starting with the header.

ToolingStudy 1 was organized in Figma. Study 2 moved to Adobe XD, which keeps images at their original size and exports layer names as JSON.

Header

noun · template sectionDEF. 01

The top portion of the page, consisting of a combination, but not exclusively, of:

  1. Site logo / title
  2. Navigational menu (links and/or dropdowns that lead to a new page)
  3. Search bar
  4. Login / user area

Headers usually have a visual delimitation: a line, shadow, or color difference. In code, it's the entire area inside the <header> section.

Insights and the scaling problem

How can we make this process scalable?

The question after study 2

The second test took months to complete. All categorization, data organization and analysis was done by hand. My approach was to look for a classification model we could train on our hand-made examples, but image recognition models are usually trained on a single label at a time, not multiple labels like we were doing.

In a third, smaller study, each selection box also carried its coordinates, so the extracted metadata looked like this:

Form; Function; Component; Template taxonomy; Width; Height; Position X; Position Y
